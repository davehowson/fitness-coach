# Foundations: What Makes a Good Training Program

> Research agent output · Topic 01 · 2026-10-08

## Executive summary

- A good program is a **fit between plan and person**: it serves the goal (specificity), delivers enough stimulus (progressive overload), stays within recovery capacity (fatigue management), progresses in a defined way, fits real constraints, and is **sustainable**. [S5][S6][S9]
- Core principles to encode in every plan: specificity, progressive overload, SRA, individualization, variation (managed, not random), fatigue management, reversibility, minimum effective dose. [S5][S6]
- **Baseline floor (public health):** 150–300 min/week moderate aerobic (or 75–150 vigorous, or equivalent mix) PLUS muscle-strengthening of all major muscle groups, moderate or greater intensity, on ≥2 days/week. [S1][S2] Anything below this is a "some is better than none" start, not a target. [S1][S2]
- **Dose-response:** more weekly hard sets per muscle generally gives more hypertrophy, with diminishing returns; strength saturates at lower volume than size. Beginners respond to small doses. [S8][S9][S10][S11]
- **Minimum effective dose is low:** ~1 hard set/exercise, 2–3×/week, near failure, produced significant (suboptimal) strength gains in trained men; maintenance of strength is possible for months on ~1/3 to 1/9 of prior volume if load/intensity is kept. [S10][S12][S13]
- **Adherence beats optimality.** Autonomous/intrinsic motivation and enjoyment predict long-term exercise; supervised, enjoyable, schedule-feasible programs get done. A "perfect" plan that is skipped loses to a good plan that is done. [S14][S15][S16]
- Before writing a plan, collect: goal, days, session length, experience, equipment, injuries, health screen, age/sex/special population, recovery context, preferences. A concrete checklist is below.
- Design top-down: needs analysis → goal → structure (days/split) → variables → progression → review/deload. [S5][S6]
- Judge finished plans with the QC checklist below (goal match, volume in range, frequency, balance, progression, deload, time fit, substitutions, safety).
- The 2009 ACSM resistance-training stand is superseded by a 2026 ACSM overview-of-reviews stand; full prescription tables of the 2026 stand could not be retrieved (see Research log). [S3][S4]

## Core concepts

### Generic → specific: the principles

| Principle | Definition | How it shows up in a plan |
|---|---|---|
| **Specificity** | Adaptations are specific to muscle actions, velocity, range of motion, muscle groups, energy systems, intensity and volume trained. [S6] | Choose exercises, rep ranges, and conditioning type that match the goal (heavy low-rep for max strength; moderate reps near failure for size; running/cycling for aerobic capacity). |
| **Progressive overload** | Without increasing demand, the body has no reason to adapt further. [S6] | Every plan states how load, reps, sets, or density increases over weeks (e.g., add reps → add load). |
| **SRA (stimulus–recovery–adaptation)** | Training is a stimulus that creates fatigue; adaptation happens in recovery, then the next stimulus is timed appropriately. Practitioner framework. | Spacing of sessions per muscle (typically 48–72 h hard-to-hard), rest days, sleep/stress-aware volume. |
| **Individualization** | Prescription depends on the person's training status, goals, constraints, and response. [S3][S5][S6] | Same goal gives different volume/frequency/complexity for beginner vs advanced; adjust to injuries, equipment, time. |
| **Variation** | Planned change of variables over time to keep progress and avoid overtraining. [S6] | Rep/load rotation, exercise variants, mesocycles. Don't vary so much that progress can't be tracked. |
| **Fatigue management** | Accumulated fatigue masks performance and raises injury/overreach risk. Practitioner consensus. | Proximity-to-failure limits, deloads, volume caps, hard-day spacing. |
| **Reversibility** | Gains regress with cessation; strength is retained better and longer than muscle size or strength endurance. [S12] | Plan for breaks: maintenance dose, realistic re-entry (retraining takes ~½ the layoff, range ⅓–⅔). [S12] |
| **Minimum effective dose (MED)** | The smallest dose that produces a meaningful result. [S10][S13] | Offer a "bare minimum" version of any plan for time-crunched users. |
| **Adherence / sustainability** | The plan must be done to work. [S14][S15] | Enjoyable exercise choice, feasible days/duration, low friction. |

### What an effective program is (position stands)
- ACSM 2009 stand (now superseded, historically important): progression requires individualization, specificity, overload, variation; sequence large-muscle/multi-joint exercises first; loads of 8–12RM for novices, 1–12RM periodized for intermediate/advanced; increase load 2–10% when 1–2 reps above target are achievable; frequency 2–3 d/wk novice, 3–4 intermediate, 4–5 advanced. [S3]
- ACSM 2011 stand (Garber): a regular program including cardiorespiratory, resistance, flexibility and neuromotor exercise is essential for most adults; ≥150 min/week moderate exercise; minimize sedentary time. [S7]
- ACSM 2026 stand (overview of 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants): evidence-graded resistance-training prescription for healthy adults; trade press summary says progressive overload is the most important variable and that as little as 3 exercises once or twice weekly yields meaningful results. [S4]
- Kraemer & Ratamess (2004): build a session from needs analysis (muscles, injury-prevention, metabolic demands) then choose exercise selection, order, rest, reps/sets, intensity; vary over time. [S6]

## Key findings

1. **Public-health floor: aerobic.** Adults need 150–300 min/week moderate, or 75–150 min/week vigorous, or equivalent combination; more than 300 min gives additional benefit; bouts need not be ≥10 min (US 2nd ed.). **Strong** (multiple national/WHO guidelines agree). [S1][S2]
2. **Public-health floor: strength.** Muscle-strengthening of moderate or greater intensity, all major muscle groups, ≥2 days/week (verified in the US guidelines [S2]; the WHO 2020 abstract confirms regular muscle strengthening for all ages, and the "≥2 days" figure for WHO was recalled from knowledge, not verified against the full WHO text in this session [S1]). **Strong** (≥2 d/wk is consistent across US/WHO/ACSM).
3. **Sedentary time:** both WHO and ACSM recommend reducing sedentary behavior, but evidence was insufficient to set a numeric threshold. **Strong** for direction, none for number. [S1][S7]
4. **Hypertrophy dose-response:** In a meta-analysis of 15 studies, each added weekly set per muscle was associated with ~0.37% larger gain; higher vs lower volume ≈ +3.9% gain difference; categories (<5, 5–9, 10+ sets) showed only a trend (P=0.074). Few studies in trained people. **Moderate.** [S8]
5. **Higher volume still helps trained men for size, not strength:** trained men doing 1, 3, 5 sets/exercise (≈6–9, 18–27, 30–45 weekly sets/muscle) for 8 weeks, 3×/wk to failure: strength gains were similar; hypertrophy was greater at higher volume (e.g., mid-thigh +3.4% vs +12.5%). Session length ≈13 vs 40 vs 68 min. Small RCT, 8 weeks. **Moderate** (single RCT). [S9]
6. **Newer meta-regression (preprint, not peer reviewed in the version seen):** positive volume–hypertrophy slope; secondary reports say strength plateaus around ~3 fractional sets/week and frequency matters more than extra sets in one session for strength. **Contested/Practitioner reading** (only secondary summaries seen). [S11]
7. **Minimum effective dose for strength:** systematic review of resistance-trained men (squat, bench): 1 set of 6–12 reps at 70–85% 1RM, 2–3×/week, high effort, 8–12 weeks gave modest but significant gains; no data on deadlift, women, or highly trained athletes. **Moderate.** [S10]
8. **Maintenance:** strength/size in young lifters maintained up to ~32 weeks with 1 session/week and ~1 set/exercise if relative load stays high; older adults may need ~2 sessions/week and 2–3 sets. Bickel et al.: young maintained on 1/9 volume, older on 1/3 (older may lose some muscle). **Moderate** (narrative review + small trials). [S12][S13]
9. **Detraining:** in younger adults strength is largely unaffected for up to ~28 days off, then declines; older adults lose roughly twice as much (g 0.76 vs 0.31); strength endurance falls sooner; retraining takes ≈½ layoff length (range ⅓–⅔). **Moderate** (meta-analysis + expert synthesis). [S12]
10. **Motivation and adherence:** in a 66-study systematic review, autonomous motivation, competence satisfaction and intrinsic motives predicted participation; identified regulation predicts adoption; intrinsic motivation predicts long-term adherence. **Moderate** (observational-heavy). [S14]
11. **Enjoyment:** enjoyment/positive affect during exercise is linked to adherence, though pooled effect sizes could not be confirmed in this session. **Practitioner/Moderate.** [S15]
12. **Supervision and adherence:** supervised programs show better adherence than self-monitored in reviewed populations (sarcopenic obesity review; small-group cohort), but evidence is limited and population-specific. **Contested/limited.** [S16][S17]
13. **Resistance training adherence vs other modes:** mixed. One review found lowest adherence to resistance training among exercise types in a narrow clinical population; a gym study found higher. **Contested.** [S16][S17]
14. **Screening:** the 2023 PAR-Q+ is the current official first-pass screen. ACSM's algorithm (per secondary guides, GETP 10th ed.+) is based on current activity level, known cardiovascular/metabolic/renal disease, signs/symptoms, and intended intensity. An asymptomatic inactive adult without known disease generally may start light–moderate activity without clearance. **Moderate** (secondary sources; verify against GETP). [S18]

## Decision rules

**Floor and goal**
- IF the person is sedentary or restarting THEN start below the floor (e.g., 2 strength sessions + walking) and build toward 150 min/wk aerobic + ≥2 strength days. [S1][S2]
- IF the person's goal is general health THEN the plan must at least meet the floor: ≥150 min moderate aerobic (or equivalent) and ≥2 whole-body strength days. [S1][S2]
- IF the plan is for hypertrophy/strength/sport THEN still check the floor is met (cardio dose can be small but should not be zero unless the user declines). [S1][S2]

**Time and MED**
- IF the user has ≤2 days or ≤30 min/session THEN use a minimum-effective-dose design: whole-body, 1–3 compound exercises per session, 1–3 hard sets each, 6–12 reps near failure, ≥2×/wk. [S10][S4]
- IF the user is on a break/travel/busy period THEN offer a maintenance block: ~1–2 sessions/wk, ~⅓ volume, keep loads heavy. [S12][S13]
- IF the user is ≥60 THEN maintenance should be ≥2 sessions/wk and 2–3 sets/exercise. [S12]

**Experience**
- IF novice THEN 2–3 whole-body days/wk, 8–12 reps, simple linear progression, low volume; prioritize learning technique. [S3]
- IF intermediate/advanced THEN raise frequency/volume gradually and periodize loads (1–12RM range); expect slower progress. [S3]

**Volume**
- IF the goal is hypertrophy THEN start in the lower-middle of the volume range and increase only when progress stalls and recovery is fine; higher volume yields more size with diminishing returns. [S8][S9]
- IF the goal is strength and time is limited THEN don't pile on sets; keep sets moderate, frequency 2–3×/lift, heavy loads. [S9][S11]

**Progression**
- IF the user completes all prescribed reps at the target effort for the week(s) THEN increase load 2–10% (smaller for upper body/isolation, larger for beginners) or add reps first (double progression). [S3]
- IF performance stalls for 2+ sessions THEN check sleep/stress/nutrition, reduce volume or take a deload before changing the whole program. [S6]

**Adherence**
- IF the user dislikes an exercise/modality THEN substitute a pattern-equivalent one they like; choose enjoyment within the principles. [S14][S15]
- IF the user has missed sessions THEN shrink the plan to the MED version, not to zero; restart where the last good week was. [S12]
- IF the user has low confidence or history of dropping out THEN prefer fewer, shorter sessions, clear success criteria, and structure/accountability (supervision, scheduled sessions). [S14][S16]

**Safety / screening**
- IF the intake shows chest pain, dizziness, known cardiovascular/metabolic/renal disease, or other PAR-Q+ "yes" flags THEN advise consultation with a physician/qualified professional before starting and don't prescribe through it (this is screening guidance, not medical advice). [S18]
- IF inactive, asymptomatic, no known disease THEN start light–moderate and progress. [S18]

## Common mistakes

- Optimizing volume/splits for a person who will only train 2 days/wk (plan not feasible, adherence collapses). [S14]
- No explicit progression rule ("do 3×10") or no deload/regress option. [S3][S6]
- Skipping the intake and prescribing generic volume regardless of experience, age, injury.
- Ignoring the health floor: all-lifting plans with no aerobic activity, or cardio-only plans with no strength. [S1][S2]
- Excess variation (new exercises each week), making progression impossible to track. [S6]
- Starting beginners at advanced volume/intensity/complexity.
- Treating more as always better; ignoring recovery context. [S9]
- Plans lasting 90+ min for a user with 45 min. [S9]
- Assuming a break resets everything: strength persists for weeks; plan a ramp-back, not a restart at day one. [S12]

## Controversies & open questions

- **Volume ceiling:** dose-response for hypertrophy continues in some data (no plateau at 30–45 sets/wk in one trial [S9]) but with diminishing returns in meta-analyses [S8][S11]; the practical optimum for trained people is unresolved. **Contested.**
- **Frequency vs volume:** whether frequency matters independent of volume (see Topic on frequency); preprint claims suggest frequency helps strength [S11] but only secondary summaries were seen.
- **Resistance training adherence:** evidence mixed (see Finding 13). [S16][S17]
- **Enjoyment → adherence:** plausible and widely repeated, but effect-size data not verified here. [S15]
- **Evidence-quality criticism:** both prior ACSM stands were criticized for lacking rigorous methodology, motivating the 2026 overview of reviews. [S4]
- **MED data gaps:** no data for women, highly trained athletes, deadlift. [S10]
- **WHO strength "≥2 days" wording** not verified from the full text here; US guideline confirms it. [S2]
- **ACSM risk-factor counting** for clearance: sources disagree; verify against current GETP. [S18]

## Program design process (top-down)

1. **Needs analysis:** intake above; identify constraints, risks, floor gap. [S6]
2. **Goal definition:** primary + secondary; measurable outcome and timeframe.
3. **Structure:** days/week → split (whole-body / upper-lower / PPL by days available), session length, rest-day placement; ensure ≥2 strength days and aerobic floor.
4. **Variables:** exercise selection (movement patterns), order (large/multi-joint first [S3]), sets, reps, load/effort, rest, tempo.
5. **Progression:** state explicit rule (e.g., double progression, +2–10% load [S3]), expected pace by experience.
6. **Review:** reassess every ~4–8 weeks (pain, adherence, performance); deload or adjust; revise the plan. [S6]

## Plan quality-control checklist

- [ ] Goal-aligned (exercises, rep ranges, conditioning match specificity)
- [ ] Meets health floor (≥2 strength days all major groups; aerobic 150+ min equivalent or a stated build-up plan)
- [ ] Fits stated days and session length (time budget summed)
- [ ] Weekly sets per muscle in range for experience/goal
- [ ] Each muscle trained ≥2×/wk (when days permit) or justified
- [ ] Movement patterns balanced (push/pull, squat/hinge, core, single-leg)
- [ ] Hard sessions for the same muscles spaced (≈48 h)
- [ ] Explicit progression rule for each main lift
- [ ] Deload/regression and missed-session rule defined
- [ ] Warm-up included
- [ ] Equipment constraints respected; substitutions given
- [ ] Injury/limitation accommodations noted; screening flags handled
- [ ] Preferences and enjoyment accounted for; minimum-dose fallback version provided
- [ ] Review point/date set
